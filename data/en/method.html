1. Across 34 case files, crashes only happen through three mechanisms

① Leverage stampede (1929 / 1987 / the 2020-03-12 circuit-breaker week / 2021-05-19 / 2024-08-05 / 2025-10-10 / SK Hynix) — the shared script: leverage or crowded positions first build up to an extreme, and once any match is lit, forced liquidations become fresh sell pressure, so the price decline itself manufactures further declines. This category has the clearest advance signals: margin debt, funding rates, open interest, carry positions, and leveraged-ETF size are all measurable.

② Exogenous shock (the pandemic circuit-breaker week / tariff day / mining-region power curbs) — the trigger itself is hard to predict, but the market's amplification factor for the shock depends on pre-existing fragility: the same bad news drops a low-leverage market 3% and a high-leverage market 15%.

③ Narrative collapse (Meta's DAU / Netflix's subscriber count / NVDA's DeepSeek day / the LUNA-FTX trust chain) — the single narrative propping up a valuation is falsified by one earnings report or one balance sheet. The precursor is usually trouble hitting a peer first (Snap before Meta, UST before LUNA, FTT before BTC, Micron before SK Hynix) — when the first name in a chain runs into trouble, check the second name you're holding.

The same logic applies to the upside: the three epic single-day rallies of 2008-10-13, 2020-03-24 and 2025-04-09 were all "a policy bazooka after a crash, plus short covering" — this never happens in a calm market.

2. What can warn you is never the "trigger" — it's "fragility"

A tweet, an earnings report, a mining accident, a central bank's Sunday-night action — triggers can't be predicted. But in every major incident in the archive, fragility indicators lit up at least days to months in advance. In practice, judge using a three-layer combination:

  1. Structural fragility: leverage buildup (margin debt at a record high, annualized funding rate >50%, OI surging, leveraged ETF assets ballooning);
  2. Sentiment extremes: Fear & Greed Index at either extreme, Put/Call too low, basis overheated, valuation stretched;
  3. First crack: the first abnormally large red candle / the first circuit breaker / term-structure inversion / a peer blowing up — historically, the first day of a crash is rarely the last leg down (1929-10-24 → 28 → 29; 2020-03-09 → 12 → 16; 2025-04-03 → 04).

When all three layers line up at once, cutting exposure doesn't require predicting tomorrow's headlines.

Quantifying every event's aftermath path (the "path" strip in each expanded archive row: +21d / +63d / deepest drawdown / days to recover; classification rules: V-shaped = pre-event close regained within 42 trading days, slow repair = within 126, L-shaped = not regained in 126, wiped out = final price below 20% of pre-event, unfinished windows marked "still unfolding") and crossing it against the three mechanisms from Chapter 1, the divergence is anything but random:

① The first question decides everything: did the position break, or did the balance sheet break? Exogenous shocks and leverage stampedes destroy positioning — once forced selling clears, the asset itself is unchanged, and recoverers regain the pre-event close in a median of 15–17 trading days. Narrative collapse destroys the premise the price was built on: of 9 cases, not one produced a V-shaped rebound; 3 were wiped out (UST's peg, LUNA's mint mechanism, FTT's collateral value — the balance sheet itself died, +63d median −94%), and 4 went L-shaped (Facebook still −17.2% at +63d, META −12.4% — DAU and subscriber narratives take quarters of earnings to rebuild). A wrong price gets bought back; a dead premise doesn't.

② Exogenous shocks going 4-for-4 on V-shapes is not pure luck. The shock doesn't impair solvency, and big shocks force a policy response: after the two 2020-03 circuit breakers came the Fed's unlimited QE on March 23 (recovered in 17–19 days); the 2025-04-04 tariff shock got its pause 3 days later. Policy speed also explains the split inside the stampede group — the 2024-08-05 yen-carry unwind (BoJ soothing the next day) recovered in 3–6 days and the 2010 Flash Crash in 4, while 1929 / 1987 / 2025-10-10 never got a bazooka and all went L-shaped. One honest caveat: 2008-10-15 regained its single-day gap in 14 days yet sat at −7.1% by +63d — recovering a one-day loss is not the same as escaping a bear market.

③ A completed leverage flush is the precondition for a fast rebound — but flushing is not repair. All 7 V-shapes in the stampede group came after forced liquidations cleared in one wave (the 312 crash took 48 days to recover, but sat at +95.8% by +63d); the 4 L-shapes share structural, layer-by-layer leverage that one liquidation wave only begins to unwind — 1929 margin loans, 1987 portfolio insurance, the hidden derivatives leverage of 2021-04, the altcoin leverage of 2025-10-10.

④ The mirror holds on the upside. A policy bazooka fired at a genuine turning point sticks (2020-03-24: +28.0% at +63d; 2025-04-09: +14.7%); fired halfway down a bear market, it gets given back (2008-10-13: +11.1% in a day, fully reversed 7 days later, −13.1% at +63d — when policy can't fix a solvency problem, a huge rally is just short covering).

In practice, answer four questions on the day of a crash — ① Is there a hole in solvency (reserves / redemptions / balance sheet — if yes, don't catch the knife)? ② Has forced selling actually cleared (funding rates normalized, margin debt down, OI collapsed → stampede near its end)? ③ Will the policy toolbox open, and how fast (a clear response sharply raises V-shape odds)? ④ Can the damaged narrative be repaired by the next earnings report (yes → slow repair, no → L-shape)? This plugs into Chapter 2's three-layer fragility framework: fragility judges whether it falls; these four questions judge what happens after it falls.

Limitations of this chapter: 34 cases is a small sample, and mechanism grouping involves judgment (per-event assignments and numbers are auditable in data/path_stats.json); the classification window is 126 trading days after the event — 1929-10-29 counts as V-shaped inside the window (next-day rebound plus a repair rally within six months), but the −89% low of 1932 falls outside it, so second collapses are not captured; and only events that made history are archived, so "fell 10% and nothing happened" samples are absent (survivorship bias).

5. Does this tag system actually discriminate? (self-check — the verdict is published even when it fails)

Every event in the archive carries precursor-category tags, and both "Reference Events" and the Indicator Dashboard match on them. What gets measured here is where things go over the 63 trading days after the crash has already happened — the question this site actually exists to answer (whether a fall is coming is handled by the three vulnerability layers in section 2; what happens after it falls is the part you can act on). The method: compare the median 63-day return of events under a given tag against the unconditional median taken at the same market mix with no matching at all.

The baseline must follow the market mix rather than the blended all-archive median: crypto wipeouts (UST -99%, LUNA -99%, FTT -85%) drag the blended median to -16.2%, while the unconditional median for US index events is only -7.8% — a threefold gap. Comparing against a mixed pool makes any US-heavy tag look "milder", which is a units error, not evidence that the signal works.

How to read it: stays weaker = after this class lights up, the asset runs worse than the norm for its market over the next 63 days — the one that genuinely warrants de-risking; runs stronger = it actually gains more than the norm afterwards, historically closer to a buying window, so using it to justify de-risking is backwards; no edge = it contributes nothing to judging direction. The max-drawdown column is the risk taken over the same span — do not read direction without it. The figures recompute daily from the archive and live data — none of them are hard-coded.

6. Admission criteria (and the few that do not meet them)

The page title says ±10%, but the real threshold is tiered by market, because the same percentage differs by orders of magnitude in rarity across markets: US headline indices ≥4%, US single stocks ≥10%, crypto 24h ≥10%. The monitor sweeps 21 tickers against these thresholds every 30 minutes; a hit enters a candidate queue for manual write-up (causal chain and precursors must each carry a source — filling them in from memory is not accepted).

Why the non-qualifying ones are not simply deleted: they are genuinely important nodes in the record (the Nikkei fell 12.4% on the yen-carry unwind day; the two news-driven BTC squeezes each started a larger move), and removing them would leave a hole in the archive. But keeping them means labelling them — otherwise "complete ±10% archive" is statistically false, and every group median here rests on this list. Whether to admit an exception is a human judgement; whether to disclose it is not: events below threshold are listed automatically, so any new one that falls short surfaces on its own rather than depending on someone remembering.

7. Honest limitations

  • Fragility indicators improve win rate and position discipline — they are not a crystal ball. Endogenous liquidity stampedes like 1987 and the 2010 Flash Crash carry very weak advance signals; the only real backstop is position sizing;
  • Exchange net inflow, Glassnode's leverage ratio, and market breadth have no free programmatic data source; the dashboard marks these as "manual-check items" with a manual-lookup link attached;
  • Thresholds are set from historical percentiles and research literature; market structure drifts over time, so it's worth revisiting them against new events every quarter.
